▌ Reference 01 ▌ Specimen Brief

The brief: design an alien.

Objective

Using CAD software, design an original alien creature and prepare it for 3D printing. Your specimen must be buildable from a small number of basic 3D shapes — make every part count.

Constraints

  • Part count: minimum 5, maximum 12 parts. Count every solid and every hole.
  • Bounding box: fits inside 80 × 80 × 80 mm.
  • Holes: at least one Hole operation (a mouth, eye socket, nostril, etc.).
  • Self-supporting: stands or sits without printed supports.
  • Submission: exported as an STL file by the end of Lesson 4.
  • Original work: not a recognisable copy of an existing character (no Stitch, no Baby Yoda, no Pokémon).

What makes a good alien

01 · Strange but believable

It should feel like it could survive somewhere — even if that somewhere is weird. Give it a hint of biology.

02 · Built from simple shapes

Cylinders for legs, spheres for eyes, cones for horns. Show the parts working together.

03 · One bold idea

Three eyes? Stacked heads? Suction-cup feet? Commit to a single distinctive choice.

Part economy

Every shape should earn its place. Five clever parts beats fifteen messy ones. Before you add another shape, ask: is this telling the viewer something new about my alien?

Rubric — how you'll be graded

Band Design thinking CAD technique Print readiness
A Distinctive, original concept; thoughtful part choices; alien feels like it could exist somewhere. Confident use of move/rotate/scale, hole, group, align, workplane; precise mm dimensions. Self-supporting; no overhangs > 45°; walls ≥ 1.5 mm; STL submitted on time.
B Clear concept; mostly intentional shape choices. Uses all core tools; some minor positioning errors. Print-feasible with minor adjustments.
C Concept recognisable but generic; some parts feel arbitrary. Builds successfully but relies on a small subset of tools. Would print but with warnings (thin walls or steep overhangs).
D Incomplete or off-brief. Difficulty combining shapes; key tools unused. Not yet print-ready.

▌ Field Hazards — Print Constraints ▌

The 45-degree rule

FDM printers build models layer-by-layer from the bottom up. Any surface that overhangs at more than 45° from vertical will sag, droop, or fail without support material. For our brief: no supports allowed.

▌ Design Rules of Thumb ▌
Min wall
1.5 mm or more — thinner walls become fragile.
Min feature
Embossed details ≥ 1 mm; engraved details ≥ 0.5 mm.
Overhang limit
45° from vertical without supports.
Bridge limit
Short, flat spans only (< 5 mm).
Tolerance
0.2 mm clearance between moving or fitted parts.
First layer
Wide, flat base helps the print stick to the build plate.

▌ Technique Reference ▌

Align

Select two or more shapes, then choose how to align them on each axis: left, centre, right (X); front, centre, back (Y); bottom, middle, top (Z).

Tip · Hold Shift to multi-select before clicking Align.

Workplane

Drag the workplane onto the face of a shape to make that face the new "ground". This is how you stack and attach things at angles.

Tip · Reset the workplane (W) before saving to avoid confusion.

Mirror

Duplicate a shape and flip it across an axis. Use this for symmetrical eyes, arms, or wings — design one, mirror the other.

Duplicate

Ctrl+D copies a shape and remembers the last move you made. Press it again and the move repeats. Useful for evenly spaced spikes or rings.

▌ Reference 02 ▌ Vocabulary

Glossary.

Keep this open beside you while you work. Cross-check any term you forget.

Bounding box
The smallest 3D box that completely contains your model. Used to check overall size.
CAD
Computer-Aided Design — software for creating accurate 2D or 3D models of physical objects.
FDM
Fused Deposition Modelling — the most common 3D printing method in schools. Builds objects layer-by-layer from melted plastic filament.
Group
Combine multiple shapes into a single object. Solids merge; Holes are subtracted.
Hole
A shape marked as "Hole" subtracts its volume from any Solid it is grouped with.
Mirror
Duplicate a shape and flip it across an axis to create symmetry.
Overhang
A surface that leans outward from the layer below. Past about 45°, it will sag without support.
Primitive
A basic 3D shape — box, cylinder, sphere, cone, torus — used as a building block.
Slicer
Software that converts an STL into thin horizontal layers and prepares instructions for the 3D printer.
STL
A file format that describes a 3D model's surface as a mesh of triangles. The standard format sent to 3D printers.
Support
Temporary printed scaffolding used to hold up overhangs. Removed after printing. For our brief: not allowed.
Tolerance
The small gap (usually 0.2 mm) needed between parts so they fit together after printing.
Wall thickness
How thick the outer surface of your model is. Below ~1.5 mm, walls become fragile.
Workplane
The "ground" your shapes sit on. You can move the workplane onto any face to stack shapes at different angles.
