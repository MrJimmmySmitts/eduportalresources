Lesson 1

Lesson 1: Digital Citizen

📓 Open Workbook

What does it mean to be safe, responsible and effective online — and why does it matter beyond just you?

y7-dt-u1-l1-digital-citizen

To be able to... understand what it means to be a safe, responsible digital citizen, and describe appropriate online behaviour.

Success Criteria

Foundation

List two rights and two responsibilities you have when you're online.

Developing

Describe what appropriate online behaviour looks like in a given scenario.

Extending

Explain why digital citizenship matters for the wider online community, not just for yourself.

1
Section 1
What is a digital citizen?

A digital citizen is anyone who uses digital technology — the internet, apps, games, social platforms — to engage with the world. Just like being a citizen of a country comes with rights and responsibilities, being a digital citizen does too.

Digital RightsDigital Responsibilities
To feel safe and respected onlineTo treat others with respect online
To have your personal information protectedTo protect your own and others' personal information
To access age-appropriate content and supportTo think critically before sharing or believing what you see
To report unsafe or unkind behaviourTo speak up when you see unsafe or unkind behaviour
2
Section 2
Your digital footprint

Everything you do online — posts, comments, likes, searches, photos — leaves a trace called your digital footprint. Some of it you create on purpose (an active footprint), and some is collected without you actively choosing it, like location data or browsing history (a passive footprint).

Key idea
Digital footprints can be very hard to fully delete. Before you post, share, or comment, it's worth asking: would I be okay with a future employer, teacher, or family member seeing this in five years?
3
Section 3
Being Internet Awesome

Google's Be Internet Awesome programme uses a game called Interland to teach the core habits of good digital citizenship, organised around four themes:

  • Be Internet Smart — share with care
  • Be Internet Alert — don't fall for fake information or scams
  • Be Internet Strong — secure your accounts
  • Be Internet Kind — treat others the way you want to be treated

Lesson 2

Lesson 2: Critical Thinking

📓 Open Workbook

Not everything on the internet is true — including some very convincing, very fake websites.

y7-dt-u1-l2-critical-thinking

To be able to... critically evaluate the credibility of online sources, and identify signs of a trustworthy or untrustworthy site.

Success Criteria

Foundation

Identify two signs that a website might not be trustworthy.

Developing

Use a checklist to assess the credibility of a website.

Extending

Justify your credibility verdict using specific evidence from the site (author, domain, sources cited).

1
Section 1
Why credibility matters

Anyone can publish a website — there's no test you have to pass first. That means the internet contains brilliant, accurate information sitting right next to confident-sounding nonsense. Being a good digital citizen means being able to tell the difference before you believe or share something.

2
Section 2
A credibility checklist

When you land on a website, ask these questions:

CheckWhat to look for
AuthorIs a real person or organisation named? Do they have relevant expertise?
DomainIs it .gov, .edu, or a known organisation — or an unfamiliar .com/.org?
PurposeIs the site trying to inform you, or sell you something, or make a joke?
EvidenceDoes it link to sources, studies, or data — or just make claims?
CurrencyIs the content dated, and is it recent enough to be reliable?
Cross-checkDo other trustworthy sites say the same thing?
3
Section 3
Famous fakes: DHMO.org and the Pacific Northwest Tree Octopus

DHMO.org presents a long list of alarming "facts" about a chemical called Dihydrogen Monoxide — component of acid rain, found in tumours, causes corrosion... DHMO is, in fact, just water (H₂O), described using scary but technically true language.

The Pacific Northwest Tree Octopus site (zapatopi.net) describes an endangered octopus that supposedly lives in trees in Washington State, complete with photos and a fake conservation call to action. It is entirely fictional — a famous hoax used worldwide to test students' web literacy.

Why these sites exist
Both were built deliberately, by researchers and educators, to study and teach media literacy. Studies have found that a large majority of students who visit these sites without training rate them as "believable."

Lesson 3

Lesson 3: Managing Digital Schoolwork

📓 Open Workbook

A messy digital desktop costs you time all year — and a laptop crash with no backup can cost you everything. Today you build a system that survives both.

y7-dt-u1-l3-managing-digital-schoolwork

To be able to... organise and manage digital files and tools effectively, including logging in, organising folders, and creating shortcuts.

Success Criteria

Foundation

Log in to your school accounts without help, and know where your files are stored.

Developing

Create a clear folder structure, rename a file using a consistent naming convention, and confirm your work is backed up to the cloud.

Extending

Set up a filing and backup system that lets you find, or recover, any piece of work in under 30 seconds, and explain the logic behind it.

1
Section 1
Why file organisation matters

By the end of Year 7 you'll have created hundreds of digital files across every subject. A system you set up in week 1 — or don't — determines whether you can find last term's work in ten seconds or ten minutes.

2
Section 2
Folder structure

A simple, reliable structure for school files:

  • Top level: one folder per subject (e.g. Digital Technology, Maths, Science)
  • Inside each subject: one folder per unit or term (e.g. Unit 1 - Digital Citizenship)
  • Avoid: saving everything loose in "My Drive" or "Downloads" with no folders at all
3
Section 3
Naming conventions

A good file name tells you what's inside without opening it. A reliable pattern:

Subject_Topic_ShortDescription

❌ Unclear✅ Clear
Document1.docxDT_U1_CredibilityChecklist.docx
final final v2 ACTUAL.docxDT_U1_ExitTicket_L2.docx
Practical tip
Consistent naming also makes files sort alphabetically in a useful order — put the subject and unit first, the specific task last.
4
Section 4
Bookmarks and shortcuts

Bookmark the pages you use every lesson — your school portal, EduPortal, your Drive/OneDrive home — into a dedicated bookmarks folder in your browser, so you're never more than one click away from your work.

5
Section 5
Why this isn't just about being tidy

A folder system only protects you if a copy of your work also exists somewhere your laptop crashing, a lost USB, or a wiped Chromebook can't reach. A tidy desktop that lives on one device is still one bad day away from disappearing.

Key idea
Being a responsible digital citizen isn't only about how you treat others online — it's also about taking responsibility for your own data.
6
Section 6
Cloud sync, in plain terms

Your school Google Drive or OneDrive saves a copy of your files online automatically, as long as you're connected to the internet — this happens whether you notice it or not. Working entirely from Downloads, Desktop, or a USB stick with no cloud connection means one device failure can cost you everything in that folder.

Sync iconWhat it means
Tick or cloudBacked up — safe
Spinning arrowStill uploading — wait before closing the laptop
Red mark or "!"Failed — check it, don't ignore it
7
Section 7
Taking a screenshot

You'll use a screenshot to show your teacher your folder structure. The shortcut depends on your device:

DeviceShortcut
WindowsWindows key + Shift + S, then select the area
MacCmd + Shift + 4, then select the area
ChromebookCtrl + Show windows (the rectangle key), then select the area
