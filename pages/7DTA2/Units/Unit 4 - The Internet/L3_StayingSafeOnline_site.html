LearningCore notes

Your digital footprint

📋 Copy this into your workbook — Section 2

Everything you post, share or search online leaves a trace — this is called your digital footprint. Some information is fine to share publicly. Other information, once posted, is very hard to take back, and can be used to find you, impersonate you, or target you with scams.

What's usually OK to share vs. what to keep private

Usually OK to shareKeep private
Your first nameFull name + home address
A hobby or interestSchool name
Your favourite game or showPhone number
General opinionsPasswords, real-time location, daily routine
Rule of thumbIf you wouldn't say it to a stranger's face at the shops, don't post it somewhere strangers can see it.

Building a strong password

📋 Copy this into your workbook — Section 3

A password is a key — and a weak one is like leaving that key under the doormat. A strong password is long, unique to each account, and not something easy to guess from your profile (no names, birthdays, or pet names).

ExampleWhy
tyler2012Short, predictable — uses a real name and a year.
password123One of the most-guessed passwords in the world.
PurpleLamp$Jumping47!Long, unrelated words, mixed characters — much harder to guess.

Three habits

  1. Make it long — aim for 12+ characters; length beats complexity.
  2. Make it unique — a different password for each important account.
  3. Never share it — not with friends, not in chat, not even with someone claiming to be from a company.

LearningCore notes

Spotting a phishing message

📋 Copy this into your workbook — Section 4

Phishing is a fake message — email, text, or DM — designed to trick you into clicking a bad link, downloading something harmful, or handing over personal information by pretending to be someone trustworthy.

Common red flags

  • Urgency or pressure — "act now or lose your account!"
  • Asking for a password, code, or personal details
  • A link or sender that looks almost right, but slightly off
  • An offer that seems too good to be true

If you spot one

  1. Don't click the link or download anything.
  2. Don't reply or enter any details.
  3. Check with the real source another way — e.g. go to the website directly, not via the link.
  4. Tell a trusted adult or report it in the app.

LearningCore notes

Protecting your devices

📋 Copy this into your workbook — Section 5

Malware ("malicious software") is software designed to damage a device, steal information, or let someone else control it without permission. It usually sneaks in through a dodgy download, a phishing link, or an out-of-date app.

Only install from trusted stores

Official app stores check apps before they're listed.

Keep apps and devices updated

Updates often fix security weaknesses that malware can exploit.

Signs something's wrong

  • The device suddenly runs much slower than usual.
  • Pop-ups appear constantly, even when you're not browsing.
  • Apps appear that you don't remember installing.
  • Friends say they're getting strange messages "from you."
Key ideaIf you notice these signs, tell a trusted adult before doing anything else — don't try to fix it alone.
