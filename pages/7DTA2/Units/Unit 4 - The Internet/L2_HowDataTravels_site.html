LearningCore notes

What is a packet?

When you send anything over the internet — a message, photo or video — it is not sent as one big lump. It is broken into many small pieces called packets. Each packet is numbered, sent separately, and joined back together at the other end.

📋 Copy this into your workbook — Section 2
Why break data into packets?Small pieces travel faster, can take different paths, and if one piece is lost only that piece is re-sent — not the whole file.

LearningCore notes

What's inside a packet?

📋 Label these in your workbook — Section 3
HeaderWhere it's going (the destination address) and the packet's number in the sequence.
PayloadThe actual piece of data — part of your message, photo or video.
FooterA simple check so the other end can tell if the packet arrived undamaged.

On the envelope: the header is the address and envelope number, the payload is the puzzle pieces inside, and the footer is a quick "did everything arrive in one piece?" check.

LearningCore notes

Finding a route

Packets from the same message don't all follow the same road. At each step, a router reads the packet's destination and sends it on by the best path available at that moment — like a signpost at every junction. If one path is busy or broken, packets simply take another.

📋 Copy this into your workbook — Section 4
Packet leaves device→ Router chooses best path→ Next router…→ Arrives at destination
Key ideaTwo packets from the same photo can arrive by different routes — whichever was fastest at the time.

LearningLight touch

How do packets know where to go?

Every device on the internet has its own address, called an IP address. It works like a home address on a letter: the packet's header carries the destination address, and routers use it to pass the packet along until it reaches the right device.

The postal picture, completeYour address (so a reply can come back) · their address (in the header) · the postal network (routers passing the envelope from junction to junction).
6
LearningCore notes

Putting it back together

📋 Copy these steps into your workbook — Section 5

1. Arrive out of order

Packets reach the destination at different times and in a jumbled order.

2. Reorder by number

The header numbers let the device put them back in the right sequence.

3. Re-send if missing

If a packet didn't arrive, just that one is requested again.

Why it's reliable

Small, numbered, independent pieces that can each take any route and be re-sent mean no single failure stops your message.
