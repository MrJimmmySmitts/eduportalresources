Lesson 1

The Elements of a Game

Working definition 📋 COPY THIS
A game is a system in which players engage in an artificial conflict, defined by rules, that results in a measurable outcome. (adapted from game designer Jesse Schell)

Seven elements every game is built from

Objective

The clear goal the player is working toward — what does "winning" or "success" look like? Can be fixed (Chess) or open/player-defined (Minecraft).

Rules

The boundaries of the game — what actions are allowed, forbidden, or required. Rules apply equally to every player; that's what makes a game fair.

Mechanics

The specific actions the player can perform — the verbs of the game. Usually a handful of verbs, combined in many ways.

Challenge

The obstacles, difficulty, or opposition that stand between the player and the objective. Without challenge, the objective feels meaningless.

Narrative

The story, characters, or theme that gives context and meaning to play. Optional, not mandatory — puzzle/arcade genres often skip it.

Aesthetics

The look and sound of the game — art style, colour, animation, music, sound effects. Shapes mood before a player reads a word.

Feedback

How the game communicates to the player — score counters, sound cues, animations, health bars. Tells players the result of their action immediately.

Worked example: Among Us

ElementHow Among Us handles it
ObjectiveCrewmates: complete tasks or vote out impostors. Impostors: eliminate crew unnoticed.
RulesImpostors can fake tasks; only the impostor can eliminate; voting is majority-based.
MechanicsMove, complete task, call meeting, vote, sabotage, vent (impostor only).
ChallengeCrew must spot suspicious behaviour with limited information; impostor must deceive.
NarrativeA spaceship crew hunting a hidden threat — light sci-fi theme.
AestheticsSimple, colourful, cartoon-style avatars keep the tone light despite the tense premise.
FeedbackTask progress bar, emergency meeting alarm, coloured vote results, ejection animation.

Analysing Games

From describing to critiquing Naming an element (Lesson 1) is not the same as judging it. A critique makes a call about an element and backs it up with evidence and effect on the player — it doesn't just describe what's there.

Why compare CONTRASTING games?

📋 COPY THIS

A contrasting pair shares a genre or purpose but makes different design choices — comparing them shows how each choice changes the player's experience. Comparing two unrelated games gives shallow, obvious answers; comparing two games in the same genre forces a much sharper comparison, because most elements start out similar and the differences are the interesting part.

Good contrasting pairs

PlatformersSuper Mario Bros.vsCeleste
Battle RoyaleFortnitevsPUBG
PuzzleTetrisvsDr. Mario
RacingMario KartvsForza Horizon

The critique sentence frame

📋 COPY THIS
[Game A]'s [element] works better than [Game B]'s because [evidence], which means [effect on the player].

Worked line: "Celeste's challenge works better than Super Mario Bros.' because it offers an optional assist mode, which means players of very different skill levels can still reach the same objective."

Worked example: Super Mario Bros. vs. Celeste

ElementSuper Mario Bros.Celeste
ObjectiveReach the flag at the end of each level.Reach the top of the mountain, level by level.
RulesOne hit from most enemies ends the attempt.Unlimited retries — death has almost no cost.
MechanicsRun, jump, stomp — simple, fixed toolkit.Run, jump, climb, air-dash — dash adds precision options.
ChallengeFixed difficulty — no way to ease a hard section.Optional Assist Mode adjusts speed/invincibility.
NarrativeRescue the princess — minimal, functional.A story about anxiety and self-doubt woven through levels.
AestheticsBright, blocky, iconic 8-bit sprites.Pixel art with a muted, moody colour palette.
FeedbackCoin sound, life counter, simple death jingle.Death counter per screen — reframes failure as progress.
Celeste's Challenge works better for a wider range of players than Super Mario Bros.' because it offers an optional Assist Mode, which means players who'd otherwise quit can still experience the full narrative.

Generating Game Ideas

Ideation 📋 COPY THIS
Ideation is the process of deliberately generating ideas using a technique or constraint — instead of waiting for inspiration to strike. The "blank page problem" makes unlimited choice paralysing; structured techniques force new, unexpected combinations.

Method 1: Word Wheels

Spin three separate wheels — Theme, Setting, and Mechanic — and force the results together into one concept. Two students spinning the exact same three results can still design completely different games: the wheel gives raw ingredients, not the finished design.

THEMEUnderwater · Space · Horror · Farming · Mythology · Cyberpunk · Post-Apocalyptic · Fantasy

SETTINGAbandoned city · Floating islands · School · Desert · Space station · Ocean depths · Small town · Ancient temple

MECHANICStealth · Racing · Building · Puzzle-solving · Platforming · Trading · Survival · Rhythm

Method 2: The Mash-Up Method

Take two existing games you know well and combine one element from each into something new. Push for combining actual mechanics, not just visuals — a surface-level mashup ("it looks like X but is Y") is weaker than one that fuses how the games actually play.

Game AGame BResult
ChessBattle RoyaleA shrinking board where pieces are eliminated as the arena shrinks.
MinecraftRhythm gamesBuilding blocks in time with a beat — mistakes break your structure.
Among UsRacing gamesA hidden saboteur is secretly slowing down one racer's car.

Method 3: "What If" Constraints

Take something players normally take for granted and remove or flip it. The constraint forces new mechanics to solve the problem it creates — e.g. "no undo" pushes toward permadeath mechanics and careful-decision gameplay.

  • What if the player couldn't see more than one metre ahead?
  • What if every action was permanent — no undo, no retry?
  • What if two players had to share one controller, one input each?
  • What if the game got easier the longer you played badly?

Worked example: spinning the wheels

THEME: SpaceSETTING: FarmMECHANIC: Racing

→ Concept: "Orbit Harvest" — rival space-farmers race hover-tractors around a shared asteroid field to reach fertile plots first.

ElementOrbit Harvest
ObjectiveReach and claim the most fertile plots before rivals.
MechanicsBoost, drift, scan for fertile plots, plant.
ChallengeLimited fuel forces risky shortcuts through asteroid debris.
FeedbackFuel gauge, claimed-plot counter, rival position radar.

Notice: only a handful of new details had to be invented once the three wheel results were combined.
