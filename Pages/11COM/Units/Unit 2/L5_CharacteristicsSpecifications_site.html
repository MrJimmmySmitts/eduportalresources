Explicit Explanation · D3.2 Novelty vs Innovation

Being new is not the same as being innovative. A solution can be completely original and still be useless — and a solution can reuse existing technology in a new context and be genuinely innovative.

Innovation = new (or newly applied) idea + genuine value for a real user, in a real context.

Novelty on its own is just difference. Innovation requires that difference to actually improve something for someone. This is why "characteristics of creative and innovative solutions" is a checklist of four separate qualities — a solution needs more than one of them to count as genuinely innovative.

Why this matters for your project

Your group already has a refined problem statement and — if you've started collecting data — some early evidence about your user's needs. Today you'll use that evidence to test whether your emerging concept is actually innovative, then turn the strengthened idea into a first draft specification.

2
Explicit Explanation · Characteristics 1 & 2 Originality & Effectiveness
1

Originality

Does this idea bring something genuinely new — a new feature, a new combination of existing technologies, or a new way of applying something familiar to this specific problem? It does not need to be a world-first. It needs to be original for your user, in your context.

Test: has someone already solved this exact problem, for this exact user group, in this exact way?

Low originality

"Students forget equipment for class, so we'll put a lost-property box at the front of the room."

Higher originality

"We'll build a peer buddy-check-in system, backed by a shared reminder app, that catches the gap before class starts — not after."

2

Effectiveness

Does the idea actually solve the problem named in your refined problem statement — for the user you scoped in Lesson 4? An original idea that doesn't address the real need fails this characteristic.

[USER GROUP] need a way to [ACCOMPLISH GOAL] because [SPECIFIC INSIGHT]

Quick self-test

Finish this sentence about your own idea: "My idea is effective because it lets [user] achieve [goal] by ___." If you can't complete the blank with something concrete, effectiveness is your weakest characteristic today.

Explicit Explanation · Characteristics 3 & 4 User-Centred & Disruptive Potential
3

User-Centred

Is the idea built around evidence about what your user actually said, does, or struggles with — or around what your group assumed they'd want?

Assumption-centred

"We think students would like reminders, so we're adding a notification feature."

User-centred

"Three of our five interviewees said they forget equipment because they pack the night before — so our reminder needs to trigger at the morning check point, not the night before."

If you haven't collected data yet

You can still apply this characteristic using your predicted answers from Lesson 4 — but flag clearly in your workbook that this is a prediction, not evidence.

4

Disruptive Potential

Does the idea change how people actually do something — or does it just bolt a small feature onto an existing routine? Disruptive does not have to mean huge or expensive. It means the underlying behaviour or process shifts.

Low disruptionHigher disruption
Adds a reminder on top of an unchanged routine.Removes the need for the user to remember at all, by shifting the check to a point they can't skip.
Digitises an existing paper process exactly as-is.Changes who is responsible for a step, or when it happens, not just what medium it's recorded in.

Extending

A strong concept rarely scores well on all four characteristics on the first attempt. Identifying your weakest characteristic honestly — and proposing one specific, concrete change to strengthen it — is more valuable than claiming a perfect score.

Explicit Explanation · D4.1 From Characteristics to Specifications

Rating your concept tells you whether it's a good direction. A specification turns that direction into something a group can actually build against — a shared, precise description of what the solution must do and be, so every group member is building toward the same thing.

Four parts of a specification

Today you'll draft the first version of all four: functional requirements (what it does), non-functional requirements (how well it does it), constraints (real limits on the project), and scope (what's in and what's deliberately left out).

6
Explicit Explanation · D4.1 Functional vs Non-Functional Requirements
Functional requirementNon-functional requirement
What the solution must do — a specific action or feature.How well the solution performs — a quality that applies across many features.
"The system must send a reminder notification at a time the user sets.""The reminder must arrive within 10 seconds of the scheduled time."
"The system must let a user mark a checklist item as complete.""The interface must be usable by someone with no prior experience within 2 minutes."

Common non-functional categories

Performance (speed, responsiveness), usability/accessibility (can the target user actually use it easily), reliability (does it work consistently), and security/privacy (is user data protected). You don't need all four today — pick the ones that matter most for your specific concept.

Writing a precise functional requirement

A vague requirement like "the app should be good with reminders" can't be built against directly — two different developers would build two different things. A precise one names the trigger, the action, and the outcome: "when the check-in time is reached and a buddy hasn't confirmed, the system must send both users a notification."

7
Explicit Explanation · D4.1 Constraints & Scope

Constraints

Constraints are real limits on your project — not design choices, but things imposed on you. Common constraints for a group project like this: the devices and software actually available at school, the time remaining before Outcome 1 submission, and the programming or design skills currently within your group.

Constraints shape decisions — they don't excuse them

Naming "we don't have much time" as a constraint is only useful if you can say what it means for a specific decision — for example, choosing a simpler notification method because building a full mobile app isn't realistic in the time available.

Scope

Scope defines the boundary of your solution — what version you're actually committing to build. This is the same "IS / IS NOT" thinking you used for your target user in Lesson 4, applied now to features instead of people.

In scope

A shared reminder that notifies both buddies at a set check-in time.

Out of scope (for now)

Automatically detecting whether equipment is actually packed (would need extra hardware) — noted as a possible future extension instead.

Being explicit about what's out of scope isn't a weakness — it protects your group from quietly running out of time trying to build everything at once.
