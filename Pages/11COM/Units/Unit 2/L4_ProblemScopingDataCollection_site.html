D2.2 · Problem Scoping Refining the Problem Statement

Your D2.1 investigation gave you a starting point — a rough problem statement based on your topic area. In D2.2, you sharpen that statement so it is specific enough to actually guide your research and eventual solution.

The Formula

[USER GROUP]  need a way to  [ACCOMPLISH GOAL]  because  [SPECIFIC INSIGHT ABOUT THE PROBLEM]

Each part does specific work:

  • [USER GROUP] — a specific, named group with shared characteristics. Not "people" or "students" — year level, context, and relevant circumstance.
  • [ACCOMPLISH GOAL] — the task or outcome your solution will help with. Concrete and actionable, not vague.
  • [SPECIFIC INSIGHT] — the because clause. This is where most problem statements fail. A strong because clause describes the mechanism of the problem: why the goal is currently difficult or impossible for this user group, in this specific context.

What Makes a Strong "Because" Clause?

The because clause should contain new information — something that isn't obvious from the goal alone. It should describe a real constraint, circumstance, or pattern that your investigation uncovered.

❌ Weak — no new insight

"Young people struggle with organisation because organisation is hard."

This is circular. It tells us nothing useful about the user or the context.

✅ Strong — specific causal insight

"Year 11 students who hold part-time jobs need a way to plan study blocks around variable work rosters because they can't predict their schedule more than 3 days ahead, making long-range planning unreliable."

Paper Notes Reminder

Your refined problem statement was written on paper during the Explicit phase of this lesson. Section 1 of your workbook is where you transfer it digitally. The formula above is the reference — don't copy it verbatim into your answer.

D2.2 · Problem Scoping Defining Your User — Who's IN and Who's OUT

Core Principle

Designing for everyone = designing for no one. Inclusion and exclusion are deliberate design decisions, not accidents. A well-scoped target user makes every design choice clearer.

Who IS Your Target User?

A specific target user description includes:

  • Age range or year level — e.g. "Year 11 students aged 16–17"
  • Context — where and when they would use your solution
  • Relevant circumstance — what makes them distinct from the general population (e.g. "holds a part-time job", "commutes to school", "is a primary carer")
  • Access and capability level if relevant — digital literacy, device access, language

Who is NOT Your Target User?

Naming at least one group your solution is not designed for is not discriminatory — it is good design practice. It forces you to make an explicit decision about scope, and prevents your solution from becoming unfocused.

Example: who is OUT (and why)

"Not primary school students — the interface assumes Year 10+ reading comprehension and self-directed use."

Example: why drawing the line is useful

If your solution is student-facing, excluding teachers clarifies that you don't need a teacher dashboard, admin features, or class-management tools. That's a significant scope reduction.

D3.1 · Data Collection Design Primary vs Secondary Research
Secondary Research Primary Research
What it is Using data that already exists — reports, articles, statistics, product reviews Data you collect directly from real users or observations
When to use it To understand the context before you design your instruments; to supplement primary findings To understand your specific users' needs, experiences, and behaviours — this lesson's focus
Examples Government stats, academic papers, app store reviews, industry reports Interviews, surveys, observations, usability tests
Limitation May not reflect your exact user group; data may be outdated Requires access to actual users; instruments must be carefully designed

Today's Focus

D3.1 is about designing primary data collection instruments. Secondary research continues in parallel as you develop your SRS — but today, you're designing the tools that let you hear directly from your users.

D3.1 · Data Collection Design Interviews vs Surveys — Side-by-Side
Interviews Surveys
Best when… You need depth; you want to follow up on interesting answers; your user group is small or hard to survey You need breadth; responses are more honest without social pressure; you need data from many people
Type of data Rich, detailed, qualitative — stories, explanations, unexpected themes Scalable mix of quantitative (rating scales, counts) and qualitative (open-text boxes)
Access needed Time with individual users — can be remote (video call). Scheduling is the main constraint. Only a link or printed form — asynchronous. Easy to distribute; hard to ensure quality responses.
Main limitation Takes longer per participant; interviewer bias — you might unconsciously guide the answer Low response rate if too long; no ability to follow up on interesting answers
Risk to watch You might lead the conversation without realising — especially with questions that imply an answer A single leading or ambiguous question skews every single response — instrument design is critical
Also consider Observation — watching users perform a task in context. Captures what users actually do, not just what they say. Harder to structure formally; use an observation checklist.

Survey Length Matters

Research on survey completion consistently shows that response rates drop sharply when surveys take more than 5–7 minutes to complete. Aim for the minimum number of questions that will give you the data you need — not the maximum your topic allows.

D3.1 · Data Collection Design Designing Good Questions

Open vs Closed Questions

TypeDescriptionBest forLimitation
Closed Fixed options: yes/no, rating scales (1–5), multiple choice Spotting patterns across many respondents; easy to graph Misses nuance; forces users into your categories
Open Free text — respondent writes their own answer Understanding why; uncovering unexpected themes Harder to summarise; requires more time to analyse

Rule of thumb: you need both types. Closed questions reveal patterns; open questions reveal reasons. A survey with only closed questions tells you what but not why.

Leading Questions — What They Are and Why They Ruin Data

A leading question is one that guides the respondent toward a particular answer — often by embedding an assumption, expressing your opinion, or using loaded language. Leading questions don't reveal the user's view; they confirm yours.

❌ Leading — embeds your assumption

"Don't you find it frustrating when apps are slow to respond?"

Why it's leading: "Don't you find it frustrating" assumes they do. The word "frustrating" is a loaded emotion the researcher chose, not the user.

✅ Neutral — lets the user respond freely

"Describe your experience when an app takes a long time to respond."

Why it works: No assumed emotion. Open-ended. The user might say frustrated, annoyed, fine with it, or something you didn't expect.

Asking About the Real Problem — Behaviour Anchoring

Vague questions get vague answers. Anchoring questions to specific behaviours or situations produces more honest, detailed, and useful responses.

❌ Vague — abstract and unmeasurable

"What problems do you have with organisation?"

✅ Behaviour-anchored — specific and concrete

"Walk me through how you tracked your deadlines during the last two weeks of term."

Minimum Requirements for Instruments

MethodMinimum requirements
Survey At least 5 questions — mix of open and closed; at least one of each type
Interview guide At least 4 questions plus 2 follow-up prompts (e.g. "Can you tell me more about that?")
Observation checklist At least 5 specific behaviours or situations to record; must be observable, not inferred

🔵 Extending — Evaluating Your Instrument

Before you finalise your instrument, read it back as if you are the user receiving it. Ask:

  • Is there any question that implies what answer I "should" give?
  • Does every question make sense to someone who doesn't already know what my solution does?
  • Is there a hidden assumption (e.g. "assumes they have a smartphone", "assumes they read English fluently") that doesn't apply to all users in my target group?
  • If I could only keep one question, which one would give me the most useful data? Why?
