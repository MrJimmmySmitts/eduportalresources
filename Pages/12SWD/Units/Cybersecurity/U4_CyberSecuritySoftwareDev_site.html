LESSON 1

Why secure software development matters

LEARNING OBJECTIVE To be able to explain what medium and large organisations are trying to achieve, and why the way software gets developed affects whether they achieve it.
KEY KNOWLEDGE Goals and objectives of medium and large organisations · advantages and disadvantages of developing software in-house or externally

Goals and objectives

Every organisation exists to do something, and everything it builds is meant to serve that purpose. Three terms are used, and they are not interchangeable.

TermWhat it means
Vision statementA description of the organisation as it would look in a future successful position — where it is trying to end up.
Mission statementThe organisation's purpose: what it is trying to achieve. For most companies the mission includes making a profit; for a non-profit it is usually providing a service to members.
GoalAn anticipated result the organisation intends to reach. A good goal is specific, measurable, achievable and timely.
ObjectiveThe smaller, achievable tasks undertaken in order to accomplish a goal.
COPY THIS A goal is the destination; objectives are the steps taken to get there. Security practices support goals — they are not a goal in themselves. An organisation does not aim to "be secure"; it aims to keep operating, keep customers, and stay within the law, and security is how it protects those things.

Why insecure development threatens the goals

Insecure development environments are a problem for any organisation that develops software, whether the work is done internally or handed to another company. The consequences reach well past the technical:

  • Financial — the cost of the incident itself, remediation, downtime and lost sales.
  • Reputational — customers who leave, and customers who never arrive because of what they read.
  • Legal and regulatory — notification obligations, investigations and penalties.
  • Operational — staff spending months fixing something instead of building the next product.

Developing in-house versus externally

Organisations choose between building software with their own staff, or contracting another organisation to build it. Both choices carry security consequences.

ApproachAdvantagesDisadvantages
In-house Full control over the development environment and its security; deep knowledge of the organisation's own systems and data; intellectual property stays inside; changes can be made quickly without renegotiating a contract. Requires hiring and retaining skilled developers; the organisation carries the whole cost of tools, training and infrastructure; a small team may lack specialist security expertise.
External Access to specialist skills without hiring them permanently; often faster to start; costs are known up front; the external provider may hold security certifications the organisation could not achieve alone. Less visibility and control over how securely the work is actually done; sensitive data and source code are shared outside the organisation; dependence on the provider for fixes and patches; intellectual property and confidentiality must be handled by contract, such as a non-disclosure agreement.
Exam framing. Case studies rarely ask "which is better". They ask which suits this organisation, given its size, its data, its budget and its goals. Answer with reference to the organisation in front of you.

LESSON 2

Vulnerabilities and risks I — technical

LEARNING OBJECTIVE To be able to describe the technical vulnerabilities present in insecure development environments, and the risks each one creates.
KEY KNOWLEDGE Types of vulnerabilities and risks within insecure development environments, including: use of application programming interfaces (APIs) · malware · unpatched software · poor identity and access management practices · man-in-the-middle attacks
COPY THIS — the three terms A vulnerability is a weakness. A threat is something that could exploit that weakness. A risk is the combination of how likely the exploit is and how bad the consequences would be. Keeping these apart is worth marks in every question on this area of study.

Application programming interfaces (APIs)

An API lets one piece of software call another. Using APIs increases the potential for exploitation, because every API is another doorway into a system — and one the organisation often does not control.

  • An API with weak or missing authentication lets an attacker call it directly rather than through the app.
  • An API that returns more data than the app displays leaks information to anyone who inspects the response.
  • An API without rate limiting can be called repeatedly to extract data in bulk or to overwhelm the service.
  • A third-party API means trusting another organisation's security as if it were your own.

Malware

Malware is malicious software designed to infiltrate and damage computer systems without authorisation. It takes advantage of weaknesses in a host computer or operating system.

TypeHow it behaves
VirusMalicious code that reproduces itself on a computer; often arrives as an email attachment or download and infects the active host.
WormA standalone, self-replicating program that spreads across systems by exploiting operating system vulnerabilities — no user action needed.
Trojan horseMasquerades as or hides inside useful software; requires a user to activate the installation, and creates a back door into the computer.
Logic bombLies dormant until a timer or a conditional trigger goes off.
RansomwareLocks or encrypts systems and data until a ransom is paid.
SpywareCollects information without the user's knowledge; typically arrives with a free download.
KeyloggerRecords keystrokes and sends passwords or sensitive information to an attacker.
Back doorAllows remote access to a system, bypassing all normal security processes.

In a development environment specifically, malware is most dangerous when it reaches a build server or a developer's machine — because from there it can be signed, packaged and shipped to customers as part of a legitimate release.

Unpatched software

Unpatched software leaves known security flaws unaddressed. This is a particularly serious vulnerability because the flaw is published: once a patch is released, the details of what it fixes are usually public, so attackers know exactly what to look for on systems that have not applied it.

  • Operating systems and servers left on old versions.
  • Development tools, build servers and CI pipelines that nobody has updated.
  • Third-party libraries and dependencies — often dozens or hundreds per project.

Poor identity and access management

Poor identity and access management allows unauthorised users to reach sensitive data and critical systems. Common failures:

  • Shared accounts, so no action can be attributed to a person.
  • Accounts that are never disabled when staff or contractors leave.
  • Everyone granted administrator rights because it is easier than managing roles.
  • No multi-factor authentication, so a single stolen password is enough.
  • Passwords reused across work and personal services, exposing the organisation to credential stuffing.

Man-in-the-middle (MITM) attacks

A man-in-the-middle attack is a type of eavesdropping attack in which communications and data are exposed to an unauthorised third party. The two parties believe they are talking directly to each other; in fact an attacker sits between them, able to read and sometimes alter what passes through.

  • Where it happens: unsecured or spoofed public Wi-Fi, compromised routers, or any connection not using a secure protocol.
  • Why it matters in development: a developer pulling code or pushing a release over an unprotected connection can have credentials captured or code altered in transit.
  • What prevents it: encryption in transit (TLS/HTTPS, SSH), certificate validation, and not working on sensitive systems over untrusted networks.

LESSON 3

Vulnerabilities and risks II — people and process

LEARNING OBJECTIVE To be able to describe the vulnerabilities that come from people and from the way an organisation works, rather than from its technology.
KEY KNOWLEDGE Insider threats · cyber security incidents · risks present from software acquired by third parties · ineffective code review practices · combined development, testing and production environments

Insider threats

Insider threats come from people the organisation trusts: current employees, former employees, contractors, vendors and business partners. They are difficult to detect precisely because the access being used is legitimate.

KindDescription
Malicious insiderDeliberately takes data, damages systems or sells access — often motivated by money or grievance.
Negligent insiderNo intent to harm, but bypasses controls for convenience: shares a login, emails a file home, disables a security check to meet a deadline.
Compromised insiderA legitimate account taken over by an outsider through phishing or stolen credentials. The organisation sees a trusted user; it is not one.

Reducing insider risk: least-privilege access, separation of duties so no single person can act unchecked, logging and monitoring of privileged activity, and a genuine offboarding process that revokes access on the last day.

Social engineering

Social engineering relies on the manipulation of human nature to persuade victims to hand over personal or sensitive information, or to grant access. At its most basic it is a confidence trick, and no amount of technology fully prevents it.

TechniqueWhat it involves
PhishingPretending to be a reputable person or entity to induce disclosure of sensitive information.
Spear phishingPhishing tailored to a specific individual, using details that make it convincing.
Smishing / vishingThe same approach delivered by SMS or by voice call.
PharmingRedirecting users to false websites that imitate a legitimate URL.
ImpersonationCreating a fictional persona and playing that role to deceive or defraud.
Credential stuffingReusing passwords harvested from one service against another, exploiting people who reuse them.
Shoulder surfingObserving password or code entry directly, sometimes with a camera.
TailgatingFollowing an authorised person through a secure door before it closes.

The main defence is training — many threats rely on inadvertent user cooperation, so users who recognise the pattern are the control. Restricting company email and SMS to business purposes, requiring unique passwords, and limiting financial authority all reduce the damage a successful attempt can do.

Software acquired from third parties

Software from third parties creates risk because users depend on the developer or distributor to supply necessary patches, improvements and modifications. The organisation inherits somebody else's decisions.

  • A dependency that stops being maintained leaves a permanent, unfixable vulnerability.
  • A compromised library ships malicious code straight into your product — and your customers trust your name on it.
  • Most projects use far more third-party code than code written in-house, and few organisations keep an inventory of what they actually depend on.
  • Open-source components are free to use and modify, but "someone will have checked it" is an assumption, not a control.

Ineffective code review practices

Ineffective code review allows security flaws and coding errors to go undetected until they are in production. Review fails in recognisable ways:

  • Reviews that are approvals in name only — clicked through without reading.
  • Senior developers exempt from review, so the least-checked code is the most powerful.
  • Reviews that look only at whether the code works, never at whether it is safe.
  • No automated static analysis to catch what humans reliably miss.
  • Changes merged directly to the main branch, bypassing review entirely.

Combined development, testing and production environments

When development, testing and production run on the same systems, every mistake made while building software can reach real users and real data.

  • Experimental or unfinished code can reach customers before it is ready.
  • A test that deletes or corrupts data destroys live data instead of test data.
  • Developers need broad access to build software — in a combined environment that access extends over live customer records.
  • An attacker who compromises the weakly protected development tooling lands directly in production.
  • Copying live customer data into testing "so the data is realistic" recreates the same exposure even when the environments themselves are separate.

LESSON 4

Security controls

LEARNING OBJECTIVE To be able to describe the six security controls used to protect software development practices and the data stored within applications, and explain what each one does and does not protect against.
KEY KNOWLEDGE Security controls used to protect software development practices and data stored within applications, including: version control and code repositories · robust identity and access management · encryption · code review · regular updates and patches to software · separated development, testing and production environments
COPY THIS — the six controls
  1. Version control and code repositories
  2. Robust identity and access management
  3. Encryption
  4. Code review
  5. Regular updates and patches to software
  6. Separated development, testing and production environments

1. Version control and code repositories

Version control records each stage of development so it is possible to roll back to any previous point. That makes it a recovery tool — but it is a security control for a further reason: every change is attributable to a named person and a point in time.

  • Malicious or accidental changes can be identified and reversed.
  • The history shows who changed what and when, which supports investigation after an incident.
  • Repository access controls determine who can read and who can push code.
  • Secure protocols (SSH, HTTPS) protect code in transit; signed commits verify that a commit really came from who it claims.
Limitation. Version control records what happened; it does not prevent it. A developer with push access can still commit a back door — the history will simply show that they did.

2. Robust identity and access management

Identity and access management ensures that only authorised individuals reach a resource, and only the resources their role actually requires.

  • Authentication — proving who you are. Multi-factor authentication adds a second factor so a stolen password is not sufficient on its own.
  • Authorisation — deciding what you may do. Permissions are granted by role, following the principle of least privilege: the minimum access needed for the job.
  • Accounting — logging user activity so unauthorised attempts can be detected and traced.
  • Lifecycle — access is granted at onboarding, adjusted when roles change, and revoked at offboarding.

3. Encryption

Encryption strategies start from the assumption that data will eventually be exposed, and so make it unintelligible to anyone not authorised to read it.

  • At rest — data stored in databases, on file systems, on backups and on laptops.
  • In transit — data moving across networks, protected with secure protocols such as TLS.
  • Key management — the generation, distribution, storage and rotation of keys. Encryption is only as strong as the protection around its keys.
Three limits worth remembering. Encryption does not help if the attacker has stolen a valid key; if the attacker is using a legitimate logged-in session, since the data is decrypted for them; or if the data is exported somewhere unencrypted, such as a plain CSV on a shared drive.

4. Code review

Code review acts as a security control because examining source code identifies — and therefore allows the elimination of — vulnerabilities before deployment.

  • Peer review of every change before it merges into the main codebase.
  • Static application security testing (SAST) — "white box" testing that analyses source, byte or binary code for vulnerabilities without executing the application.
  • Dynamic application security testing (DAST) — "black box" testing that checks outputs for known inputs against a running application.
  • Dedicated security reviews for critical sections such as authentication, payments or data export.

5. Regular updates and patches

Deploying updates and patches is a critical component of software security, because it closes flaws that are already publicly known. Effective patch management includes a documented policy with a maximum time to apply critical patches, an inventory of what needs patching, verification that patches were correctly applied, and regular vulnerability scanning to find what was missed.

Worked example — WannaCry (2017). The WannaCry ransomware spread worldwide by exploiting a Windows vulnerability for which Microsoft had released a patch some weeks earlier. Organisations that had applied the patch were unaffected; organisations that had not — including hospitals running critical systems — lost access to their data. The vulnerability was not unknown. The failure was in patch management, which is a process, not a technology.

6. Separated development, testing and production environments

Separating the three environments acts as a security control because each stage is isolated from the others.

EnvironmentPurposeWho should have access
DevelopmentWhere code is written and experimented with. Broken states are expected.Developers
TestingWhere code is verified against requirements before release. Uses realistic but non-live data.Developers and testers
ProductionThe live system that real users depend on.A small, named set of operations staff

Separation can be physical or logical, and should be supported by formal deployment procedures for moving code from one environment to the next, plus change management logs recording that those procedures were followed.

Which control addresses which vulnerability

VulnerabilityPrimary controlSupporting control
Poor identity and access managementRobust IAM (MFA, least privilege)Access logging and periodic review
Unpatched softwareRegular updates and patchesVulnerability scanning
Man-in-the-middle attacksEncryption in transitSecure repository protocols
Insider threatsRobust IAM and least privilegeVersion control attribution; code review

LESSON 5

Threat modelling principles

LEARNING OBJECTIVE To be able to apply threat modelling principles to an organisation's software development practices.
KEY KNOWLEDGE Threat modelling principles, including: defining security requirements · identifying and mitigating threats · confirming threats have been mitigated
COPY THIS — definition Threat modelling is a structured process in which an organisation identifies the threats to its systems and data, judges how serious each one is, plans actions to reduce them, and then checks that those actions worked.

Four things follow from that definition:

  • It is deliberate, not accidental. Threats are written down and rated, rather than dealt with only when something breaks.
  • It involves the whole organisation. Judging "the worst that could happen" is a value judgement, and managers, developers and operations staff weigh the same threat differently. Involving a cross-section builds a shared understanding of risk.
  • It produces priorities, not a wish list. No organisation can fix everything at once.
  • It is never finished. Threats change, so the model is revisited.

Reactive security versus threat modelling

REACTIVEPROACTIVE
Incident happens → damage discovered → emergency fix → customers notified → a control is added afterwards.

Cost is highest here: the breach has already happened, and the organisation pays for the fix, the downtime and the notification.		 Assets and requirements defined → threats listed and rated → mitigations chosen by priority → mitigations tested → the model reviewed as things change.

Cost is lowest here: fixing a design flaw before release is far cheaper than fixing it afterwards.

Industry frameworks

Several established threat modelling frameworks exist. You are not expected to memorise their internals — you are expected to know they exist and what they have in common.

  • OWASP — the Open Worldwide Application Security Project; community-maintained guidance focused on web application security.
  • Microsoft STRIDE — a general checklist of threat categories applied to any software design.
  • VerSprite PASTA — Process for Attack Simulation and Threat Analysis; frames threats in business terms.
COPY THIS — what all frameworks share
  • A description of the security requirements
  • Assumptions that can be re-checked as threats change
  • Identification of potential threats
  • Actions that mitigate each threat
  • Validation of the model, and confirmation that action taken was successful

Principle 1 — Defining security requirements

Defining security requirements means identifying and establishing the controls and measures needed to protect a system, so that security is designed in from the beginning rather than added later.

Step A — Identify the assets

  • Data — customer records, payment details, source code
  • Applications — the customer-facing app, the admin portal
  • Systems and infrastructure — servers, repositories, build pipelines

Step B — Identify the stakeholders

  • Users of the system
  • Administrators and developers who hold access
  • Business owners who carry the legal and financial risk

Step C — Write the requirement so it can be tested

QualityExample
WEAK"Customer data must be kept safe." — untestable; nobody can prove it has been met.
STRONG"Customer payment data must be encrypted at rest and in transit, and readable only by the payments service account."

Prioritising and reviewing

  • Prioritise by impact and likelihood. High-priority requirements protect assets whose loss would seriously harm the organisation, where that loss is plausible.
  • Resources are limited. Not all requirements can be delivered at once, so they are sequenced.
  • Review continuously. Threats evolve and systems change, so requirements are revisited on a schedule.

Principle 2 — Identifying and mitigating threats

Threats to data integrity fall into three categories, and each calls for a different kind of response.

CategoryDescriptionExamplesResponse
ACCIDENTALCaused by people making mistakes. Note that mistakes rarely "just happen".User inattention; confusing look-alike interfaces; no confirmation before a live change; permissions letting untrained users delete.Better design and training
EVENT-BASEDExternal events, largely outside the organisation's control.Storage failure; power failure; fire, flood or lightning; cloud or third-party outage; file corruption.Contingency planning
DELIBERATESomeone is actively trying to cause harm.Malware and ransomware; hackers, insiders and criminals; social engineering; DoS and DDoS attacks.Active defences

Contingency planning for event-based threats

Because these cannot be prevented, the organisation prepares recovery instead:

  • Hot site — a full or partial duplicate of the primary operation with near-real-time backups; switchover in a few hours. Used when little or no downtime can be tolerated.
  • Warm site — in between: limited in scope, relying on restored backups being brought online before services resume.
  • Cold site — may take weeks to establish; the business operates at a reduced level in the meantime.
  • Plus regular full and incremental backups, RAID, diagnostic monitoring and an uninterruptible power supply.

Rating a threat

Threats are rated by combining likelihood with consequence.

Consequence ↓ / Likelihood →UnlikelyPossibleLikely
MajorMediumHighExtreme
ModerateLowMediumHigh
MinorLowLowMedium
  • A rare event with catastrophic consequences can outrank a weekly nuisance.
  • Extreme risks are escalated to management — they are not a developer's decision to make.
  • The rating is a judgement, so the reasoning behind it is recorded.
  • A control already in place changes the rating: a stolen laptop is a different consequence entirely if the disk was encrypted.

Four responses to a rated threat

StrategyWhat it meansExample
REDUCEPut a control in place that lowers the likelihood or the consequence.Enforce MFA on the repository; encrypt the customer database.
AVOIDStop doing the risky thing at all.Stop storing card numbers; use a payment gateway that holds them instead.
TRANSFERMove the financial consequence to someone else.Cyber insurance; a hosting contract carrying uptime penalties.
ACCEPTConsciously decide to live with it, and record that decision.A two-minute outage during the nightly deploy window is tolerated.
Ignoring a threat is not the same as accepting it. Acceptance is documented, dated and approved by someone with the authority to carry the consequence — and revisited when circumstances change.

Principle 3 — Confirming threats have been mitigated

Putting a control in place is a claim. Confirmation is the evidence. Six processes are used:

ProcessWhat it involves
Security assessmentsVulnerability scanning and penetration testing on a schedule, finding weaknesses before an attacker does.
Monitoring and loggingContinuous monitoring of network traffic, system activity and user behaviour; logging allows incidents to be traced and audited.
Patch managementVerifying that patches were applied, applied correctly, and did not introduce new problems.
Access control reviewsPeriodic checks that permissions still match job roles, with unnecessary privileges revoked.
Security awareness trainingRegular training and simulations so staff recognise and respond to threats.
Security auditsInternal or external audits of the overall security posture and compliance with standards.

Intrusion detection and prevention systems (IDS/IPS), firewalls and antivirus software add layered defence, and their alerts are themselves evidence that monitoring is working.

Worked example — a threat model

Asset: source code repository.  Requirement: only current developers can push code, and every change is attributable to a named person.

ThreatTypeRatingMitigationConfirmation
A departing developer keeps repository accessDeliberate (insider)HighOffboarding checklist revokes access on the last dayQuarterly access review compared against the HR staff list
Credentials stolen by phishingDeliberate (social engineering)ExtremeMFA enforced on all repository accountsLogin logs show 100% MFA; simulated phishing test each semester
Developer pushes a secret key into the repositoryAccidentalMediumAutomated secret scanning blocks the commitScan report shows zero secrets in the last 90 days of commits

Every entry in the Mitigation column is one of the six security controls from Lesson 4. Threat modelling is how you choose which one, and where.

When a threat model is updated

  • A new feature is released — new code means new attack surface, new data and new permissions.
  • A security incident occurs — the incident proves the model missed something.
  • Organisational or infrastructure change — staff turnover, a new provider, a merger or a new office all change who has access to what.
Assumptions are the weak point. Every threat model rests on assumptions — "our hosting provider patches their servers", "the payment gateway is secure", "nobody outside the office can reach the build server". These are recorded so they can be re-checked, because a model built on an assumption that quietly stopped being true is worse than no model at all: it creates false confidence.

Three mistakes to avoid

  1. Using threat, vulnerability and risk interchangeably. The vulnerability is the weakness; the threat is what could exploit it; the risk is how likely that is combined with how bad it would be.
  2. Stopping at "add a control". Naming a control without saying which threat it addresses, why it was prioritised, or how you would confirm it works leaves most of the marks on the table.
  3. Treating confirmation as optional. It is one of the three named principles — an answer with no evidence step is missing a third of the key knowledge.

LESSON 6

Evaluating security practices

LEARNING OBJECTIVE To be able to propose criteria for evaluating an organisation's software development practices, and apply them to reach a judgement.
KEY KNOWLEDGE Criteria for evaluating the security of software development practices within an organisation
KEY SKILL Propose and apply criteria to evaluate the effectiveness of the current software development practices

Why criteria are used

ReasonExplanation
Consistency and standardisationClear criteria give a repeatable approach across different projects and teams, so all aspects of security are assessed uniformly and nothing is overlooked because one team was judged more gently than another.
Proactive risk identificationSystematically evaluating practices against criteria surfaces risks and vulnerabilities before they lead to incidents, rather than after.
Benchmarks for complianceCriteria provide a clear point of comparison against industry standards and regulations, which matters most for organisations handling sensitive data.
Continuous improvementCriteria are reviewed and updated as threats emerge, keeping security measures effective and building a culture of security awareness.

Without criteria, an evaluation is just an opinion — and an opinion cannot be defended to a board, an auditor, or a court.

The four parts of a criterion

COPY THIS
  1. Practice — which security control is being evaluated
  2. Criterion — a closed question about that practice, answerable with evidence
  3. Data to collect — the specific records, logs or documents you would gather
  4. Measurement — what you do with that data to reach a judgement

The test: could two different people gather the same evidence and reach the same answer? If not, it is a wish, not a criterion.

Worked example — writing a criterion

ATTEMPT 1  "Code should be reviewed properly." — nothing to collect, nothing to measure, and "properly" means whatever the reader wants it to mean.

PartContent
PracticeCode review
CriterionDoes every code change undergo peer review before it is merged into the main branch?
Data to collectPull request logs from the repository for the last 90 days, showing reviewer approval on each merge
MeasurementCount merges with a recorded approval from someone other than the author, as a percentage of all merges. Below 100% is a finding.

The measurement produces a number. Numbers can be compared over time; adjectives cannot.

A criterion for each practice

PracticeSample criterionData to collect
Version control and repositoriesAre only authorised personnel able to access the code repositories?Repository audit logs showing permissions and activity
Robust identity and access managementIs MFA enforced on every account with access to development tools?Authentication logs confirming MFA status per account
EncryptionIs sensitive data encrypted both at rest and in transit?Database and network configuration files; protocol logs
Code reviewAre automated static analysis tools run on the codebase?Static analysis reports and records of issues resolved
Regular updates and patchesIs there a documented policy for applying security patches within a set time?Patch policy document; records of patch application timelines
Separated dev / test / productionAre the three environments physically or logically separated?Network diagrams and access control policies

One criterion per practice is a starting point, not a finished evaluation — a real evaluation uses several per practice, and each needs its own measurement.

Applying criteria — from evidence to judgement

  1. Collect — gather the data your criterion named: logs, policies, configurations, records.
  2. Measure — do what the measurement says. Count, compare, inspect.
  3. Judge — MET PARTIALLY MET NOT MET, with the evidence attached to the judgement.
  4. Recommend — every "not met" or "partially met" becomes a recommendation, ranked by risk.
Worked judgement — code review criterion
Evidence: Of 168 merges in the last 90 days, 141 carried a reviewer approval. All 27 unreviewed merges came from the two most senior developers.
Judgement: Partially met — 84% coverage, with a consistent exemption pattern at senior level.
Recommendation: Enforce review at the repository level so approval cannot be bypassed by seniority; re-measure next term.

Notice that the recommendation addresses the cause the evidence revealed — seniority bypass — rather than restating the criterion.

If the evidence does not exist, that is a finding. If nobody can produce a list of who has production access, the criterion is not "unmeasurable" — it is failed, and the recommendation writes itself.

What makes a recommendation worth writing

  1. Anchored in evidence — it names the finding it responds to. "Because 27 merges bypassed review…", not "because security is important".
  2. Specific and actionable — someone could be assigned it on Monday. "Improve security culture" cannot be assigned to anyone.
  3. Prioritised — ranked by the risk it addresses and what it costs.
  4. Re-measurable — it names how you will know it worked, which returns you to the criterion you started from.
The loop closes: criterion → evidence → judgement → recommendation → re-measure against the same criterion.

Easy to measure versus worth measuring

Criteria sets drift toward what is easy to count. "Is antivirus installed on every developer machine?" is trivially measurable; "would a developer recognise and report a phishing email targeting the release pipeline?" is much harder to measure but far closer to how organisations actually get breached.

The consequence is important: an organisation can pass an evaluation while remaining genuinely insecure, if the criteria set never looks at the things that matter. A high "percentage met" measures the criteria set as much as it measures the organisation.

Three mistakes to avoid

  1. Writing a control where a criterion belongs. "Use MFA" is a control — an action. "Is MFA enforced on every account with development access?" is a criterion — a question answerable with evidence.
  2. Proposing criteria but never applying them. The key skill says propose and apply. A criteria table with no evidence, no measurement and no judgement has done half the task.
  3. Recommendations that float free of the findings. If a recommendation could have been written before the evaluation started, the evaluation did not inform it.

LESSON 7

Legislation and industry frameworks

LEARNING OBJECTIVE To be able to explain the key legislation and industry frameworks that affect how organisations develop software and control the security and communication of data.
KEY KNOWLEDGE Key legislation and industry frameworks, including the: Copyright Act 1968 (Cwlth) · Essential Eight · Information Security Manual (ISM) · Privacy Act 1988 (Cwlth) (APP 1, 6, 8, 9, 11) · Privacy and Data Protection Act 2014 (IPP 1, 2, 4, 5, 9)
Legislation tells you what you must do. Frameworks tell you how to do it well. Breaching legislation carries penalties; ignoring a framework does not — but a framework is where an organisation's security criteria and recommendations usually come from.

Copyright Act 1968 (Cwlth)

The Copyright Act protects the rights of creators of creative and artistic works. Computer software is treated as a literary work under the Act, which means source code is protected the moment it is written — there is no need to register it.

  • Copyright protects the expression of an idea, not the idea itself. It does not cover ideas, procedures or facts — so a competitor may legally reimplement your concept in their own code.
  • Copyright in a photograph belongs to the photographer, not the subject. When an AI tool generates an image, the prompt author is treated as owning the copyright.
  • The Act permits making a backup copy of software you own, and allows a further backup if the first is destroyed.
  • The Act was amended in 2001 to cover works published electronically, and again in 2006 to give clearer direction to users.
  • Penalties are substantial — the Act allows for a maximum of 850 penalty units for some offences, and aggravated damages are available under section 115(4).

Why it matters when developing software

  • Using third-party code, libraries or assets without complying with their licence is a breach.
  • Open-source licences are copyright licences — "free to use" comes with conditions, and some require you to release your own code.
  • Code copied from a forum, a tutorial, or generated by an AI assistant trained on copyrighted material carries unresolved licensing questions.
  • Distributing through an app store means agreeing to that store's terms as well: revenue share, acceptable content, and how many devices a copy may be installed on.

Privacy Act 1988 (Cwlth)

The Privacy Act gives an individual control over the way their personal information is handled. It allows individuals to know why their information is being collected and who it will be disclosed to, to request access to it, to have incorrect information corrected, to opt out of direct marketing, and to make a complaint if their information has been mishandled.

Who it covers: Australian government agencies, all private health service providers, and businesses with an annual turnover above $3 million or that trade in personal information. Smaller businesses can opt in. It does not cover local councils or state governments — Victoria has its own Act, below.

COPY THIS — the five examinable Australian Privacy Principles
APPTitleWhat it requires
APP 1Open and transparent management of personal informationManage personal information openly and transparently, including maintaining a clearly expressed and up-to-date privacy policy.
APP 6Use or disclosure of personal informationSets out the limited circumstances in which an entity may use or disclose personal information it holds — generally only for the purpose it was collected.
APP 8Cross-border disclosure of personal informationSets out the steps that must be taken to protect personal information before it is disclosed overseas.
APP 9Adoption, use or disclosure of government related identifiersOrganisations are generally prohibited from adopting a government identifier — a tax file number, a driver's licence number — as their own identifier for a person.
APP 11Security of personal informationTake reasonable steps to protect personal information from misuse, interference, loss, and unauthorised access, modification or disclosure — and destroy or de-identify it when it is no longer needed.
APP 11 is the one that drives this entire area of study. "Reasonable steps" is not defined in the Act, so an organisation demonstrates it through exactly the artefacts you have been building: security controls, a threat model, criteria-based evaluation, and evidence that mitigations were confirmed. This is why a documented evaluation matters legally, not just technically.

Privacy and Data Protection Act 2014 (Victoria)

The PDPA is Victorian legislation. It replaced the Information Privacy Act 2000 and applies to Victorian government agencies, including local councils and contractors working for the state government. It created the Privacy and Data Protection Commissioner, and uses its own Information Privacy Principles (IPPs) rather than the Commonwealth APPs.

IPPTitleWhat it requires
IPP 1CollectionCollect only the information the organisation actually needs, and inform people that their information is being collected.
IPP 2Use and disclosureUse and disclose personal information only for the purpose it was collected for, or a secondary purpose a person would reasonably expect.
IPP 4Data securityProtect information from misuse, loss, unauthorised access, modification or disclosure, and take reasonable steps to destroy or de-identify information no longer needed.
IPP 5OpennessBe transparent about what the organisation does with information. Non-compliance carries a maximum penalty of 3000 penalty units for a body corporate and 600 for an individual.
IPP 9Transborder data flowsIf personal information travels outside Victoria, its privacy protections must travel with it.

A penalty unit is a fixed dollar amount set by the Victorian Government and indexed each financial year, so legislation can specify penalties without naming a figure that would date.

Telling the two Acts apart. If the case study organisation is a private company, you are in Privacy Act / APP territory. If it is a Victorian government agency, a council, or a contractor working for one, you are in PDPA / IPP territory. A company that builds software for a Victorian department can be caught by both.

The Essential Eight

The Essential Eight is an industry framework published by the Australian Cyber Security Centre (ACSC), designed to help all businesses become cyber secure and resistant to cyber attack. It recommends a risk-based approach in which each organisation sets minimum expectations for preventative measures at a chosen level of maturity.

COPY THIS — the eight mitigation strategies
  1. Application control — allow only approved executables to run
  2. Patch applications — update software to address known vulnerabilities
  3. Configure Microsoft Office macro settings — prevent malicious macros running
  4. User application hardening — limit browser and application functionality to reduce attack surface
  5. Restrict administrative privileges — limit who holds administrative access
  6. Patch operating systems — keep operating systems current with security patches
  7. Multi-factor authentication — require verification beyond a password
  8. Regular backups — back up critical data so it can be recovered after an incident

Maturity levels

LevelWhat it means
Level ZeroThere are weaknesses in the organisation's posture.
Level OneFocused on preventing access by malicious actors and mitigating simple exploits.
Level TwoPrepared for weak authentication and phishing exploits by actors intent on stealing or destroying data.
Level ThreePrepared for organised actors who exploit unpatched software, circumvent authentication controls and disguise their activity inside the organisation.

Maturity assessment is self-assessed and not externally accredited. An organisation chooses the level it wants to reach, and how it gets there. The levels encourage continuous improvement rather than a pass/fail judgement.

Useful for the SAC. The Essential Eight reads almost directly as a criteria set. A recommendation phrased as "adopt Essential Eight Maturity Level Two, beginning with multi-factor authentication and application patching" is specific, benchmarked against an industry standard, and re-measurable — which is exactly what a strong recommendation needs.

The Information Security Manual (ISM)

The ISM is also published by the ACSC and provides more detailed guidance than the Essential Eight. Its cyber security principles are grouped into four activities:

ActivityWhat it covers
GovernIdentifying and managing security risks — risks are identified, documented and managed before and during deployment.
ProtectImplementing controls to reduce risk — only trusted users, systems, applications and code are permitted; access is restricted; data is encrypted, controlled and backed up.
DetectCollecting and analysing event logs, and analysing security events to identify incidents.
RespondReporting incidents internally and externally, containing them, and enacting recovery.

ISM Guidelines for Software Development

The study design names three ISM sections specifically. Recommended criteria for software development environments include:

  • Development, testing and production environments — segregated areas for each, to limit the spread of malicious code.
  • Secure software design and development — protection of software source; secure-by-design and secure-by-default principles; use of memory-safe programming languages such as C#, Go, Java, Ruby, Rust and Swift.
  • Application security testing — testing applications for vulnerabilities before and after release.

LESSON 8

Ethics and improving practices

LEARNING OBJECTIVE To be able to discuss the ethical issues that arise when developing software, and recommend strategies that improve the security of an organisation's development practices.
KEY KNOWLEDGE Ethical issues that arise when developing software, including: ineffective security practices · use of artificial intelligence during development · intellectual property · copyright issues
Mitigation measures to reduce or eliminate threats, vulnerabilities and risks · strategies for improving the security of software development practices, including: onboarding/induction practices and developer training focused on secure development · development of risk management plans

Ethics versus law

Ethics refers to behaving in ways based on our morals and accepted standards — standards that may be shared by a society or specific to a single organisation. They apply to questionable activities over and above any legal requirement. Ignoring them is not necessarily a crime, but it can end a career or a company's reputation.

COPY THIS Ethical principles are usually broader than the law. A law applies to certain circumstances; the ethical principle behind it applies generally. This is why "I have done nothing illegal" and "what you did was wrong" can both be true at once.

Legal remedies are often unavailable in practice: legislation may give no clear guidance, the cost and time of obtaining an enforceable judgment may be prohibitive, and the speed of social media and the ease of crossing legal jurisdictions have made many older laws ineffective. Many Privacy Act 1988 provisions have not kept pace with the digital environment.

Ineffective security practices

Ineffective security practices create ethical issues by exposing users to data breaches, identity theft and loss of privacy — which cause financial and emotional harm to people who had no say in how the software was built. Regardless of what the law requires, developers have an ethical responsibility to protect users' sensitive information and maintain the integrity and confidentiality of their data. Failing to do so breaks users' trust and erodes public confidence in technology generally, not just in that one company.

Use of artificial intelligence during development

  • Bias. AI systems embed the biases present in their training data, which can produce unfair or discriminatory outcomes and reinforce stereotypes.
  • Transparency and accountability. Decisions made by an AI system can be difficult to explain, which makes it hard to establish who is responsible when it goes wrong.
  • Privacy and consent. AI can process vast amounts of personal data without explicit user approval.
  • Job displacement. Automation of development work displaces the people who previously did it.
  • Code provenance. An AI assistant that suggests code trained on other people's repositories raises unresolved questions about copyright and licensing in the output.
Case study — bias in recruitment algorithms. In 2018 Amazon discontinued an AI recruitment tool that was biased against women. It had been trained on a decade of résumés submitted to a male-dominated industry, so it learned to favour résumés resembling those of male applicants — downgrading résumés containing the word "women" and penalising experience associated with female candidates.

Takeaways: training data must be diverse and representative; algorithms need regular audits for bias involving diverse stakeholders; and human oversight remains essential, because automation improves efficiency without guaranteeing fairness.

Intellectual property

Intellectual property rights can cause ethical problems by limiting access to important technologies and ideas, slowing creativity and progress. Enforced too strictly, IP rights discourage developers from collaborating and sharing, and tend toward monopolies in which a few large companies control the market — making it harder for smaller companies and individuals to compete. The ethical task is balancing protection of inventors' rights against the value of open access and shared knowledge.

Copyright issues

The Copyright Act protects original works, but does not cover ideas, procedures or facts — and that gap creates ethical dilemmas. A developer can take an uncopyrighted idea from another source, build software on it and profit substantially, while the originator receives no recognition or compensation. At the other extreme, strict enforcement restricts access to educational and cultural material, raising questions of fair use and social equity. The tension is between protecting creators and ensuring public access to information and innovation.

"Safe" programs

Programmers are legally obliged to produce programs that work properly. If a user can link harm to the work of a programmer, the programmer may be liable for the damage caused. Licences often exclude liability where software was used in a way that was not intended — but a bug that causes harm can still leave the programmer exposed.

Ethically the bar is higher than the legal one: a programmer must consider who the users are and take no shortcuts in testing, because software is written for nuclear power plants, chemical factories, medical facilities and financial institutions as well as for entertainment.

Strategies for improving security practices

Onboarding and induction

Effective onboarding helps new employees become productive and integrated into the company's culture while understanding security protocols and expectations.

  • Orientation programs — an overview of the company, its goals, values, policies and security protocols, including data protection standards.
  • Role-specific training — induction tailored to the role, emphasising the security practices relevant to it. New developers receive specialised training on secure coding, version control and the tools used in their environment.
  • Mentorship — pairing new employees with experienced mentors who guide them through their initial period, transfer knowledge and model adherence to security protocols.

Developer training

  • Regular workshops on current threats and best practice — secure coding, threat modelling, incident response.
  • Courses and certifications in cyber security and secure software development, such as the Certified Secure Software Lifecycle Professional (CSSLP).
  • Security awareness programs — updates on security trends, phishing simulation exercises, and recognition for developers who demonstrate strong practice.

Note the link back to Lesson 5: a phishing simulation is not just training, it is confirmation evidence — it measures behaviour rather than attendance.

Risk management plans

A risk management plan identifies, assesses and mitigates risks to software development and operations, so that the organisation can continue operating securely. A systematic risk assessment involves four activities:

  1. Identify and evaluate risks to determine their potential impact and likelihood — cyber security threats, legal compliance issues and operational risks.
  2. Develop and implement mitigation strategies to reduce or eliminate identified risks — for example regular software updates, employee training and improved access controls.
  3. Prepare incident response plans so that a security incident is met with defined communication protocols, containment measures and recovery procedures, minimising damage and speeding recovery.
  4. Monitor and review the plan so it stays current and relevant — checking that new threats are considered and that current staff know their roles if an incident occurs.

Rating risks in a risk management plan

Risks are assessed against a probability matrix and assigned a category, and the results identify the priority areas for management. Probability runs from Improbable to Frequent; impact runs from Insignificant to Catastrophic; the two combine to give a score from 1 to 9.

ScoreRisk ratingStatusAction
1–2LOWAcceptableNone
3LOWAcceptableRoutine audit
4–5MEDIUMTolerableAddress as soon as possible
6HIGHManageablePriority
7SERIOUSManageablePriority
8EXTREMETo be avoidedUrgent priority
9CATASTROPHICTo be avoidedImmediate action

This is the same likelihood-and-consequence reasoning used in threat modelling (Lesson 5), formalised into a plan the organisation maintains and reviews.

Where everything joins up. Threats are identified and rated (L5) → controls are selected to mitigate them (L4) → criteria are proposed and applied to judge whether they work (L6) → legislation and frameworks set the benchmark those criteria are measured against (L7) → and the whole thing is documented as a risk management plan, supported by training, so it survives staff turnover and keeps improving (L8).

SAC preparation

IN-CLASS TEST · CASE STUDY
THE TASK An in-class test in which you respond to a teacher-provided case study describing an organisation's software development practices. You will analyse those practices, identify and evaluate the security controls and threats present, and make recommendations to improve them. Closed book, completed in class, within a set time.
You will not be asked to recall the unit in the abstract. Every question will point at the organisation in the case study. Marks come from applying what you know to that organisation — its size, its data, its budget, its staff, its legal position. A technically correct answer that could have been written without reading the case study will not score well.

What the case study will contain

Expect a description of an organisation running to roughly one to two pages, containing some mixture of:

  • What the organisation does, how big it is, and what it is trying to achieve
  • What data it holds, and whose data it is
  • How its software is developed — in-house or externally, and by how many people
  • Details of its current practices, some sound and some deliberately weak
  • Possibly an incident that has already occurred, or a near miss
  • Constraints: a budget, a deadline, a shortage of staff

The weaknesses are planted deliberately. Read with a pen: underline every practice detail as you go. A shared login, a contractor who still has access, a test environment using live data, a policy nobody follows — each one is there because a question depends on it.

The five things you will be asked to do

Key skillWhat a strong response doesWhere to revise
Analyse and describe the organisation's software development practices Describes what they actually do, in the language of the six controls — not what they should do. Neutral, accurate, specific to the case. Lessons 1, 4
Identify and describe vulnerabilities and risks based on current practices Names the vulnerability, names the threat that could exploit it, and states the risk in terms of likelihood and consequence for this organisation. Lessons 2, 3, 5

Command terms — what each one is asking for

TermWhat you must do
IdentifyName it. Short answer, no explanation needed unless asked.
DescribeName it and say what it is or how it works. Add detail from the case study.
ExplainSay why or how. Cause and effect — "this happens, which means that happens".
DiscussPresent more than one side, or more than one consequence, before reaching a position.
AnalyseBreak the situation into its parts and show how they relate.
EvaluateMake a judgement against criteria, supported by evidence from the case.
JustifyGive the reasons your answer is the right one — usually why this option beats the alternatives.

The single most common error is answering a lower command term than the one asked. A question saying "evaluate" and answered with a description will lose most of its marks even if everything written is true. Circle the command term before you start writing.

Answer structures that work

For a vulnerability or risk question

Practice → Vulnerability → Threat → Consequence for this organisation

"The case states that all four developers share a single administrator login for the production server (practice). This means no action can be attributed to a person and every developer holds far more access than their role requires (vulnerability — poor identity and access management, breaching least privilege). A stolen or misused credential would give an attacker or a disgruntled insider complete control of production (threat). Because the organisation holds payment data for 40 000 customers, the consequence would be a major breach with notification obligations, and the likelihood is raised by the fact that the password has never been changed (risk)."

For an evaluation question

Criterion → Evidence from the case → Judgement → Recommendation

"Criterion: Are the development, testing and production environments separated, and is live data kept out of testing? Evidence: The case states the three environments run on separate servers, but that developers copy the live customer database into testing each month. Judgement: Partially met — the environments are separated, but the data is not, so the protection the separation was meant to provide is undermined. Recommendation: Because live records are being copied into a lower-security environment, generate anonymised test data from the production schema instead, and verify each quarter that no production records exist in the testing database."

For a legal and ethical question

Name the law and principle → State the breach → Separate the ethical issue → Say how it is resolved

"As a private company with a turnover above $3 million, the organisation is bound by the Privacy Act 1988. Storing customer records unencrypted on a shared drive is unlikely to meet the requirement under APP 11 to take reasonable steps to protect personal information from unauthorised access. Separately from the legal position, there is an ethical issue: customers supplied their details to buy a ticket, not to have them held indefinitely in an unprotected file, and they have no way of knowing the risk they are carrying. This is resolved by encrypting the data at rest, restricting access to the roles that need it, and destroying records that are no longer needed."

Managing the time

  • Read the case study twice before writing. Once for the story, once with a pen, marking every practice detail.
  • Check the marks. One mark is roughly one distinct point. A four-mark question needs four things, not one thing said four ways.
  • Quote the case. Referring to a specific detail — a number, a role, a system named in the scenario — is the fastest way to show your answer is grounded in the organisation.
  • Leave the longest question until you have banked the short ones, but do not run out of time before the recommendations — they usually carry the most marks.
  • Do not restate the case study. Describing what the organisation does earns marks only where the question asks you to describe. Elsewhere it is padding.

Six mistakes that cost marks

  1. Using threat, vulnerability and risk interchangeably. Keep them apart in every answer.
  2. Listing controls without linking them to a threat in the case. A control with no threat attached answers nothing.
  3. Proposing criteria and never applying them. The skill is propose and apply.
  4. Naming "the Privacy Act" without naming the principle. Specify APP 11, or IPP 4, and say what it requires.
  5. Recommendations that could have been written before reading the case. "Improve security awareness" is not a recommendation.
  6. Recommending everything. The organisation has a budget. Prioritise, and say what you are consciously leaving for later.
If you only revise one thing. Practise the four-step evaluation structure — criterion, evidence, judgement, recommendation — until you can write it without thinking. It answers evaluation questions, recommendation questions and most "discuss" questions, and it is the structure the whole area of study has been building toward.

Glossary

ALL LESSONS
Australian Privacy Principles (APPs)
Thirteen principles under the Privacy Act 1988 setting the standards, rights and obligations for handling personal information. APP 1, 6, 8, 9 and 11 are examinable in this study.
back door
Allows remote access to a computer system, circumventing all security processes.
cold site
Prepared external recovery infrastructure that is not immediately ready; may take weeks to resume normal operations.
copyright
The legal right of the creator of a work to determine how that work is used. Software is treated as a literary work under the Copyright Act 1968.
credential stuffing
Exploiting password reuse across different applications to gain access with credentials stolen elsewhere.
criterion
A closed question about a practice that can be answered with evidence, used to evaluate security.
cyber security principles (ISM)
The ISM's four grouped activities — govern, protect, detect and respond — providing strategic guidance on protecting systems and data.
DAST
Dynamic application security testing; "black box" testing that checks outputs for known inputs on a running application.
distributed denial-of-service (DDoS)
An attack that overwhelms a server by flooding it with requests until it slows or stops.
encryption
Rendering data unintelligible to anyone not authorised to read it, whether stored or in transit.
Essential Eight
An industry framework published by the Australian Cyber Security Centre, setting out eight mitigation strategies and four maturity levels to help businesses resist cyber attack.
ethics
Principles of right and wrong accepted by an individual or social group; broader than the law, and applying to conduct over and above legal requirements.
exploit
To take advantage of a vulnerability.
honeypot
A decoy server imitating the real system, used to lure attackers away from production and waste their time. Multiple honeypots form a honeynet.
hot site
Prepared external recovery infrastructure ready to take over immediately, with little or no downtime.
identity and access management (IAM)
The processes ensuring only authorised individuals reach resources, and only those their role requires.
Information Privacy Principles (IPPs)
The ten principles used by Victoria's Privacy and Data Protection Act 2014. IPP 1, 2, 4, 5 and 9 are examinable in this study.
Information Security Manual (ISM)
Detailed cyber security advice published by the Australian Cyber Security Centre, including guidelines for software development.
insider threat
A threat originating from someone the organisation trusts: an employee, former employee, contractor, vendor or partner.
intellectual property (IP)
Rights over creations of the mind. Enforced too strictly, IP rights can limit collaboration and access to technology.
keylogger
Records keystrokes and sends passwords or sensitive information to an attacker.
least privilege
The principle that each account is granted only the minimum access its role requires.
logic bomb
Malicious code that lies dormant until a timer or conditional trigger activates it.
malicious actor
Any individual or group that intentionally exploits vulnerabilities in systems, networks or software to cause harm.
malware
Malicious software designed to infiltrate and damage computer systems without authorisation.
man-in-the-middle (MITM) attack
An eavesdropping attack in which communications and data are exposed to an unauthorised third party.
MFA / 2FA
Multi-factor and two-factor authentication: a password plus a verification code delivered by another device or channel.
mitigation
An action taken to reduce the likelihood or consequence of a threat.
non-disclosure agreement (NDA)
A contractual agreement restricting the ability to use or share information.
onboarding and induction practices
Processes for integrating and training new employees in company culture, job responsibilities and security protocols.
penalty unit
A fixed dollar amount set by the Victorian Government and indexed each financial year, used in legislation to specify penalties without naming a figure.
pharming
Redirecting users to false websites that imitate a legitimate URL.
phishing
Pretending to be a reputable person or entity in order to induce disclosure of sensitive information.
ransomware
Malicious software that locks or encrypts systems and data until a ransom is paid.
risk
The combination of how likely a threat is and how serious its consequences would be.
risk management plan
A documented set of strategies and processes for identifying, assessing and mitigating risks to development and operations, including incident response and regular review.
risk tolerance
Also risk appetite: the quantity and nature of risk an organisation finds acceptable.
SAST
Static application security testing; "white box" analysis of source, byte or binary code for vulnerabilities without executing the application.
secure-by-design
An ISM principle: security is built into a system from the outset rather than added afterwards. Secure-by-default means the safest configuration is the one shipped.
social engineering
Manipulating human nature to persuade a victim to disclose information or grant access.
spyware
A program that collects information without the user's knowledge, usually arriving with a free download.
tailgating
Following an authorised person through a secure door before it closes. Also called piggyback entry.
threat
Something that could exploit a vulnerability and cause harm.
threat modelling
A structured process for identifying threats, rating them, planning mitigations and confirming those mitigations worked.
Trojan horse
A program that appears safe but creates a back door into the computer; requires user activation.
version control
Records each stage of development so it is possible to roll back to any previous point, and attributes every change to a person.
virus
Malicious code that reproduces itself on a computer, often arriving as an attachment or download.
vulnerability
A weakness in a system, process or practice that could be exploited.
warm site
Recovery infrastructure between hot and cold; relies on restored backups being brought online before services resume.
worm
A standalone, self-replicating program that exploits operating system vulnerabilities to spread.
